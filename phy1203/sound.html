Sound, Ultrasound and Hearing

A stethoscope, an ultrasound scan, an elephant's rumble and a bat's hunting call all use the same physics: a vibration that travels through a medium as a pressure wave.

Class 11

Vibration and the nature of sound waves

By the end you can: explain how a vibration makes sound; tell longitudinal from transverse waves; use v = fλ, T = 1/f, ω = 2πf, k = 2π/λ; read a wave equation; explain why sound is faster in bone.

11.1Vibration: where sound starts

Every sound begins with something vibrating, moving back and forth about a rest position: vocal cords, a drum skin, a heart valve snapping shut, a piezoelectric crystal in an ultrasound probe. Each push squeezes the neighbouring air (or tissue) together; each pull spreads it apart. That pattern of squeezes travels outward as a sound wave.

Describing a vibration
  • Amplitude A: the biggest displacement from rest.
  • Period T: time for one full back-and-forth (s).
  • Frequency f = 1/T: cycles per second, hertz (Hz). A heart at 72 beats/min has f = 1.2 Hz.
  • Angular frequency ω = 2πf (rad/s).

11.2What makes a wave a wave

Key concept

A wave carries energy from place to place, but not matter. Each particle of the medium just vibrates about its own spot. Think of a stadium "wave": the pattern goes round the stadium, the people stay in their seats.

  • Transverse: particles move perpendicular to the wave direction (wave on a string, light).
  • Longitudinal: particles move along the wave direction, making compressions and rarefactions. Sound is longitudinal.
Simulation

Watch a sound wave move

Try: switch between Longitudinal (sound) and Transverse. Follow the orange particle. Then change the frequency and the wave speed and read off the wavelength.

What to notice: the orange particle only jiggles about its own place; the pattern is what travels. Higher frequency squeezes the crests closer together (shorter λ), because v = fλ and v is set by the medium.

3D simulation

A sound wave in a block of air

Try: drag to rotate. The speaker cone pushes and pulls the air. Change the frequency and the loudness.

What to notice: red slices are compressions (high pressure), blue slices are rarefactions (low pressure). They march away from the speaker while each air molecule only shuffles back and forth. Louder = bigger shuffles, not a faster wave.

11.3Frequency, wavelength and speed

Key concept
v = f λ    T = 1f    ω = 2πf    k = 2πλ    v = ωk

A wave moving in the +x direction is written y(x, t) = A sin(kx − ωt). With +ωt instead, it moves in the −x direction.

Simulation

Build a wave equation

Try: change A, λ and f. The equation underneath the graph updates with real numbers, and the wave moves at v = fλ.

What to notice: A sets the height, λ sets the spacing of crests, f sets how fast each point bobs. The number in front of x is k = 2π/λ and the number in front of t is ω = 2πf.

Worked example · reading a wave

y = 0.02 sin(314t − 6.28x) m. Compare with y = A sin(ωt − kx): A = 0.02 m, ω = 314 rad/s, k = 6.28 rad/m.

f = ω/2π = 50 Hz; λ = 2π/k = 1.0 m; v = fλ = 50 m/s.

11.4Speed of sound: stiffness wins

Key concept
v = √B / ρ

B = bulk modulus (how hard the medium is to squeeze), ρ = density. Bone is denser than air, but enormously stiffer, so sound moves much faster in bone.

11.5Wave behaviour

  • Reflection: a wave bounces off a boundary. Echoes, and every ultrasound image.
  • Refraction: a wave bends when it enters a medium where its speed is different.
  • Interference: two waves add. Crest on crest gives a louder sound (constructive), crest on trough cancels (destructive). Noise-cancelling headphones use this.
  • Diffraction: waves spread around obstacles and through gaps about as big as their wavelength. You hear someone round a corner because audible sound has λ of centimetres to metres.

11.6The sound spectrum

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Below that is infrasound; above it is ultrasound. The physics is the same; only our ears decide the names.

Simulation

Sound spectrum explorer

Try: slide the frequency from 1 Hz up to 20 MHz. Switch the medium between air and soft tissue to see the wavelength change.

What to notice: each step to the right is a factor of 10 in frequency. An elephant's call has a wavelength of tens of metres; a 10 MHz scan in tissue has a wavelength of 0.15 mm, small enough to see fine detail.

Practice problems

  1. A heart beats 72 times per minute. Find (a) the period, (b) the frequency in Hz. easy

    (a) T = 60/72 = 0.833 s. (b) f = 1/T = 1.2 Hz.

  2. Soft tissue has v = 1540 m/s. Find λ for (a) 3 MHz, (b) 12 MHz. Which shows finer detail? easy

    (a) λ = 1540/3 × 106 = 0.513 mm. (b) 0.128 mm. 12 MHz: shorter wavelength gives finer resolution (about λ/2).

  3. An elephant calls at 8 Hz in air (343 m/s). Find (a) λ, (b) T, (c) the time to reach another elephant 5 km away. easy

    (a) λ = 343/8 ≈ 42.9 m. (b) T = 1/8 = 0.125 s. (c) t = 5000/343 ≈ 14.6 s.

  4. For y = 0.04 sin(6.28x − 188.5t) m, find A, λ, f, v and the direction. medium

    A = 0.04 m; λ = 2π/6.28 = 1.0 m; f = 188.5/2π ≈ 30 Hz; v = fλ = 30 m/s; moving in the +x direction (kx − ωt).

  5. What frequency gives λ = 0.30 mm in soft tissue? easy

    f = v/λ = 1540/0.30 × 10−3 ≈ 5.13 MHz.

  6. Why does sound travel faster in bone than in air, though bone is denser? easy

    v = √(B/ρ). Bone's bulk modulus is about 105 times bigger than air's, while its density is only about 103 times bigger. The stiffness wins.

Class 12

Ultrasound and infrasound: imaging, hearing aids and lab uses

By the end you can: find depth from an echo time; use Z = ρv and the reflection fraction; explain why gel is needed; use decibels; explain the depth vs detail trade-off; describe hearing aids, ultrasonic cleaning and infrasound.

12.1Ultrasound imaging: timing an echo

The probe sends a short pulse (1–15 MHz) into the body and listens. Every boundary between tissues reflects a little sound back. The machine knows v ≈ 1540 m/s and measures the round-trip time t:

Echo ranging
d = v t2

Divide by 2 because the pulse goes down and back. Bats and dolphins find prey the same way (echolocation).

12.2Acoustic impedance and reflection

Key concept
Z = ρ v    R = (Z2 − Z1Z2 + Z1)2    T = 1 − R

Z (unit rayl = kg/m²s) is how strongly a medium resists a pressure wave. R is the fraction of intensity reflected at a boundary, T the fraction transmitted. Equal Z means no reflection at all; a big mismatch reflects nearly everything.

Simulation

What happens at a boundary?

Try: choose the two materials and press Send pulse. Compare tissue → muscle with tissue → bone and skin → air.

What to notice: a small mismatch (tissue–muscle) lets almost everything through, giving a faint echo. Tissue–bone reflects about 45%, so bone shows up bright and hides what is behind it. Air reflects 99.9%: that is why the probe needs gel.

Simulation

Find the depth of a bone

Try: set how deep the bone surface is and press Send pulse. Read the echo time and check d = vt/2. Then switch the gel off.

What to notice: a boundary 5 cm deep returns its echo after about 65 µs. Without gel, the air gap reflects almost all the sound at the skin and the echo from the bone disappears.

12.3Intensity and decibels

Key concept
I = PA    β = 10 log10II0   (dB),   I0 = 10−12 W/m2

Our ears cover 12 powers of ten in intensity, so we use a logarithmic scale. +10 dB = 10× the intensity. +3 dB = 2× the intensity. Above about 85 dB, long exposure damages the cochlea's hair cells, which do not grow back. Each +3 dB halves the safe daily time (8 h at 85 dB, 4 h at 88 dB, 2 h at 91 dB…).

12.4Hearing aids

A hearing aid has three parts: a microphone turns sound into an electrical signal, an amplifier boosts it, and a small speaker (receiver) plays it into the ear canal. The boost is the gain in dB. Modern digital aids give more gain at the frequencies the person has lost (often high frequencies) and limit the output so loud sounds never reach a damaging level. For people whose middle ear cannot pass sound on, bone-conduction aids vibrate the skull directly; for severe hair-cell loss, a cochlear implant stimulates the hearing nerve electrically.

Simulation

Decibels and a hearing aid

Try: pick a sound, then raise the hearing-aid gain until the patient (who needs at least 50 dB to hear) can hear it. Don't push the output into the danger zone.

What to notice: 20 dB of gain multiplies the intensity by 100. Quiet speech needs the aid, but a loud sound plus gain quickly passes 85 dB, which is why good aids cap their output.

12.5Attenuation: depth vs detail

Key concept
I(d) = I0 e−2αd    soft tissue: loss ≈ 0.5 dB per cm per MHz

Sound fades as it goes deeper, losing a fixed fraction per centimetre (exponential, like a drug clearing from blood). Higher frequency fades faster. But higher frequency has shorter λ and so finer detail (resolution ≈ λ/2). So abdominal scans use 1–3 MHz (deep, coarse) and thyroid or vein scans use 7–15 MHz (shallow, sharp).

Simulation

Choose the probe frequency

Try: move the frequency from 2 MHz to 12 MHz. Watch how deep the beam can usefully reach and how small a detail it can show.

What to notice: there is no single best frequency. Each choice trades how deep you can see against how sharp the picture is.

Worked example · round-trip loss

A 5 MHz probe images a target 6 cm deep (12 cm round trip). Loss = 0.5 × 5 × 12 = 30 dB. Fraction returned = 10−30/10 = 10−3 = 0.1%. The machine amplifies the echoes to make up for it.

12.6Other uses of ultrasound and infrasound

UseHow it works
Medical imaging (sonography)Echo timing builds a picture; safe (no ionising radiation), used in pregnancy, heart (echocardiography), abdomen.
Doppler ultrasoundEchoes from moving blood cells come back at a shifted frequency, giving blood speed and direction.
Ultrasonic cleaning (lab)20–40 kHz waves in a liquid bath make tiny bubbles that collapse violently (cavitation), scrubbing glassware and instruments.
Cell disruption (sonication)The same cavitation breaks open cells to release DNA and proteins.
TherapyFocused ultrasound heats and destroys small tumours; lithotripsy breaks kidney stones.
Infrasound in natureElephants and whales communicate over kilometres with long, slowly-fading waves.
Infrasound monitoringDetects earthquakes, volcanoes and storms far away. Strong infrasound near 5–10 Hz can make body organs resonate, causing discomfort and nausea.

Practice problems

  1. An echo returns 130 µs after the pulse (v = 1540 m/s). How deep is the boundary? easy

    d = vt/2 = 1540 × 130 × 10−6/2 ≈ 0.10 m = 10 cm.

  2. Find Z for soft tissue (ρ = 1050 kg/m³, v = 1540 m/s) and bone (ρ = 1900 kg/m³, v = 4080 m/s). easy

    Ztissue = 1050 × 1540 ≈ 1.62 MRayl. Zbone = 1900 × 4080 ≈ 7.75 MRayl.

  3. Find the fraction reflected at a tissue (1.54 MRayl) to bone (7.75 MRayl) boundary, and the fraction transmitted. medium

    R = ((7.75 − 1.54)/(7.75 + 1.54))² = (6.21/9.29)² ≈ 0.447. T ≈ 0.553.

  4. Compute R at an air (0.0004 MRayl)–skin (1.62 MRayl) boundary, then with gel (1.50 MRayl). medium

    No gel: R ≈ 0.999 (99.9% reflected). Gel: R = (0.12/3.12)² ≈ 0.0015 (0.15%).

  5. A conversation is 60 dB and a rock concert 110 dB. How many times more intense is the concert? easy

    50 dB difference = 105 = 100,000 times.

  6. Two sources each give 70 dB. What is the level when both play? medium

    Intensities add: 2 × 10−5 W/m². β = 10 log(2 × 107) ≈ 73 dB (not 140 dB).

  7. A hearing aid has 30 dB gain. By what factor does it multiply the sound intensity? easy

    1030/10 = 1000 times.

  8. A 7 MHz probe looks at a target 10 cm deep. Find the round-trip loss and the fraction of intensity returned. medium

    Loss = 0.5 × 7 × 20 = 70 dB. Fraction = 10−7.
