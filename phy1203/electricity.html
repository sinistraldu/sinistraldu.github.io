Electricity in the Body

Every nerve signal, heartbeat and thought is electrical. Cell membranes hold charge like capacitors, ion channels act as resistors, and a neuron behaves like a simple RC circuit.

Class 13

Electric charge, Coulomb's law, electric field and field lines

By the end you can: state the properties of charge; use F = kq1q2/r²; find E = kq/r²; sketch field lines; describe an electric dipole.

13.1The nature of electric charge

Key concept
  • Two kinds: positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  • Charge is quantised: it comes in whole multiples of e = 1.6 × 10−19 C (one proton is +e, one electron is −e).
  • Charge is conserved: it can move around but is never created or destroyed.
  • Conductors let charge move (metals, salty body fluids); insulators do not (the lipid core of a membrane, myelin).

In biology the charges are mostly ions: Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cl−. DNA is negatively charged along its backbone, which is why it moves towards the positive electrode in gel electrophoresis.

13.2Coulomb's law

Key concept
F = k q1 q2r2    k = 14πε0 = 8.99 × 109 N·m²/C²

Double the distance and the force drops to one quarter (inverse square). In a medium such as water the force is weaker by the dielectric constant κ (water: κ ≈ 80), which is why salt dissolves.

Simulation

Two charges push or pull

Try: change the two charges (make one negative) and pull them apart.

What to notice: both charges feel the same size of force, in opposite directions (Newton's third law). Doubling r cuts the force to 1/4; doubling one charge doubles the force.

13.3Electric field and field lines

Key concept
E = Fqtest    point charge: E = kqr2   (N/C or V/m)

The field is the force per unit positive charge at each point in space. Field lines start on + charges and end on − charges, never cross, and are crowded where the field is strong.

Simulation

Draw the field lines

Try: pick a set-up. Drag the charges around on the picture. Turn on Show potential for the next class.

What to notice: lines leave + and enter −. Between a + and a − (a dipole) lines link the two; between two + charges they push apart and leave an empty point in the middle where E = 0.

13.4The electric dipole

Key concept
p = q d    torque in a field: τ = p E sin θ

An electric dipole is a + and a − charge separated by a distance d. Water is a dipole (p ≈ 6.2 × 10−30 C·m); so are many proteins and the heart as a whole, which is why an ECG can read it from the skin. A dipole in a field is twisted until it lines up with the field.

3D simulation

The field all around a charge

Try: drag to rotate. Switch between a single charge and a dipole.

What to notice: the field exists in all three dimensions, not just on paper. Arrows are long and bright near the charge and fade as 1/r². For the dipole they curve from + to −.

Worked example · salt in water

Na+ and Cl−, 0.28 nm apart. In vacuum: F = (8.99 × 109)(1.6 × 10−19)²/(0.28 × 10−9)² ≈ 2.9 × 10−9 N. In water, divide by κ = 80: about 3.7 × 10−11 N, weak enough for thermal motion to pull the ions apart.

Practice problems

  1. Two charges of 2 µC and 3 µC are 0.5 m apart. Find the force. Attractive or repulsive? easy

    F = 8.99 × 109 × 2 × 10−6 × 3 × 10−6/0.25 ≈ 0.216 N, repulsive (both positive).

  2. How many electrons make a charge of −1 nC? easy

    n = 10−9/1.6 × 10−19 ≈ 6.25 × 109 electrons.

  3. Find E at 10 cm from a 5 nC charge. easy

    E = kq/r² = 8.99 × 109 × 5 × 10−9/0.01 ≈ 4500 N/C.

  4. If the distance between two charges is tripled, what happens to the force? easy

    F ∝ 1/r², so it becomes 1/9 of the original.

  5. A dipole p = 6.2 × 10−30 C·m sits at 90° to a field of 107 V/m (like inside a membrane). Find the torque. medium

    τ = pE sin 90° = 6.2 × 10−30 × 107 = 6.2 × 10−23 N·m.

Class 14

Electric potential, equipotentials, capacitors and dielectrics

By the end you can: use U = kq1q2/r and V = kq/r; relate E and V; describe equipotentials; use C = Q/V and C = κε0A/d; treat a cell membrane as a capacitor.

14.1Potential energy and potential

Key concept
U = k q1q2r    V = Uq = kqr    W = q ΔV

Potential V (volts, J/C) is potential energy per unit charge. It is a number, not a vector, so potentials from several charges simply add. A charge q moved through a potential difference ΔV gains or loses energy qΔV. One electron-volt (eV) = 1.6 × 10−19 J.

14.2Equipotentials

An equipotential is a surface where V is the same everywhere. Moving a charge along it costs no work. Equipotentials are always perpendicular to field lines, and the field points from high V to low V. In a uniform field:

E = ΔVd

Go back to the field-line simulation above and switch on Show potential: the colour bands are equipotentials.

14.3Capacitors and dielectrics

Key concept
C = QV    C = κ ε0 Ad    U = ½CV2

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. It stores charge (Q = CV) and energy. Bigger plates, a thinner gap, or a dielectric (insulator with κ > 1) between the plates all give more capacitance. ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m. Unit: farad (F).

Simulation

Build a capacitor

Try: change plate area, gap and voltage, then slide a dielectric in by raising κ.

What to notice: at fixed voltage, a thinner gap or a dielectric lets the plates hold more charge. The field between the plates is uniform, E = V/d, so a 7 nm membrane with only 70 mV across it has a huge field of 107 V/m.

The cell membrane is a capacitor

The lipid bilayer (κ ≈ 7, d ≈ 7 nm) is an insulator separating two salty conductors (cytoplasm and outside fluid). C per area = κε0/d ≈ 7 × 8.85 × 10−12/7 × 10−9 ≈ 0.9 µF/cm². At a resting potential of −70 mV, each cm² holds only about 6 × 10−8 C, a tiny layer of extra ions right at the membrane surface.

Practice problems

  1. Find the potential 1 nm from a single proton. easy

    V = kq/r = 8.99 × 109 × 1.6 × 10−19/10−9 ≈ 1.44 V.

  2. How much energy does a Na+ ion gain crossing a 70 mV membrane? Give J and eV. easy

    W = qΔV = 1.6 × 10−19 × 0.07 = 1.12 × 10−20 J = 0.07 eV.

  3. Find the field inside a 7 nm membrane with 70 mV across it. easy

    E = ΔV/d = 0.07/7 × 10−9 = 1.0 × 107 V/m.

  4. Parallel plates: A = 0.01 m², d = 1 mm, air. Find C, and Q and U at 12 V. medium

    C = ε0A/d = 8.85 × 10−11 F (88.5 pF). Q = CV ≈ 1.06 × 10−9 C. U = ½CV² ≈ 6.4 × 10−9 J.

  5. A defibrillator stores energy in a 100 µF capacitor charged to 2000 V. How much energy? easy

    U = ½CV² = 0.5 × 10−4 × 4 × 106 = 200 J.

  6. How many monovalent ions sit on 1 cm² of membrane (C = 0.885 µF/cm²) at 70 mV? medium

    Q = CV = 0.885 × 10−6 × 0.07 ≈ 6.2 × 10−8 C. n = Q/e ≈ 3.9 × 1011 ions.

Class 15

Electric current, resistance, Ohm's law and EMF

By the end you can: use I = Q/t, V = IR, R = ρL/A and P = VI; explain EMF and internal resistance; treat membranes and axons as resistors.

15.1Current and resistance

Key concept
I = Qt    V = I R    R = ρ LA    P = V I = I2R

Current I (amperes, C/s) is the flow of charge. Resistance R (ohms) opposes it. Ohm's law: current is proportional to voltage for a fixed resistor. Resistance grows with length and falls with cross-section area; ρ is the material's resistivity (Ω·m). In body fluids, the moving charges are ions, not electrons.

Simulation

Ohm's law with a lamp

Try: raise the battery voltage, then raise the resistance. Watch the charges and the lamp.

What to notice: double V and the current doubles; double R and it halves. The lamp's power P = VI grows with V² at fixed R.

15.2EMF and internal resistance

Key concept
Vterminal = ε − I r

The EMF ε is the energy a source gives each coulomb of charge (volts). Real sources have an internal resistance r, so the voltage at their terminals drops when they deliver current. In cells, the "batteries" are ion concentration differences across the membrane.

15.3Biological membranes and axons as resistors

The lipid bilayer is a very good insulator; ions cross it only through ion channels. Each open channel is a tiny resistor (conductance, G = 1/R, in siemens). Opening more channels lowers the membrane's resistance. Inside an axon, the axoplasm is a salty conductor, but it is thin and not very conductive (ρ ≈ 2 Ω·m, about 100 million times copper's), so an axon is a poor wire.

Simulation

Why an axon is a poor wire

Try: change the length and radius of the "wire" and switch between axoplasm and copper.

What to notice: R = ρL/A. A thin axon a few centimetres long has hundreds of megaohms of resistance, so a signal cannot simply flow along it like in a copper cable. Nerves solve this with action potentials that regenerate the signal (Class 19).

Practice problems

  1. A charge of 30 C passes through a wire in 1 minute. Find the current. easy

    I = Q/t = 30/60 = 0.5 A.

  2. A 12 V source drives 0.2 A through a resistor. Find R and the power. easy

    R = V/I = 60 Ω. P = VI = 2.4 W.

  3. An axon is 1 cm long with radius 5 µm and ρ = 2 Ω·m. Find its resistance. medium

    R = ρL/(πr²) = 2 × 0.01/(π × 25 × 10−12) ≈ 2.5 × 108 Ω.

  4. A 9 V battery with internal resistance 0.5 Ω drives a 4 Ω load. Find I and the terminal voltage. medium

    I = ε/(R + r) = 9/4.5 = 2 A. V = ε − Ir = 9 − 1 = 8 V.

  5. A single ion channel passes 2 pA at 100 mV. Find its resistance and conductance. easy

    R = 0.1/2 × 10−12 = 5 × 1010 Ω. G = 1/R = 20 pS.

Class 16

RC circuits: charging, discharging and the time constant

By the end you can: use the charging and discharging equations; find τ = RC; explain why a membrane responds slowly to a stimulus; describe a simple RC neuron model.

Key concept
Charging: VC(t) = ε(1 − e−t/RC)    Discharging: VC(t) = V0 e−t/RC
τ = R C

A capacitor cannot charge instantly, because the resistor limits the current. After one time constant τ it reaches 63% of its final voltage (or falls to 37% when discharging). After 5τ it is essentially done. The current is largest at the start and dies away: I = (ε/R)e−t/RC.

Simulation

Charge and discharge a capacitor

Try: press Charge, wait, then Discharge. Change R or C and try again. The dashed line marks t = τ.

What to notice: the curve is steep at first and flattens out. At t = τ the voltage is 63% of the battery voltage. Doubling either R or C doubles τ, so everything happens twice as slowly.

Where the equation comes from

Step 1Loop rule while charging: ε = IR + q/C.

Step 2I = dq/dt, so dq/dt = (εC − q)/(RC).

Step 3Separate and integrate from q = 0: q = εC(1 − e−t/RC). Divide by C to get VC.

Cell membranes and simple neuron models

A patch of membrane is a capacitor (the bilayer) in parallel with a resistor (the leak channels). Inject a small current and the membrane voltage does not jump; it rises along an RC curve with τ = RmCm, typically 1–20 ms. In the simplest neuron model (the integrate-and-fire model), the membrane charges like an RC circuit and, when it crosses a threshold, the cell fires a spike and resets. Pacemakers and heart-rate monitors use the same RC timing.

Practice problems

  1. Find τ for R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 µF. easy

    τ = RC = 104 × 10−4 = 1.0 s.

  2. That capacitor charges from a 9 V battery. Find VC at t = 1 s and t = 2 s. easy

    t = τ: 9(1 − e−1) ≈ 5.69 V. t = 2τ: 9(1 − e−2) ≈ 7.78 V.

  3. How long does it take a discharging capacitor to fall to half its voltage? medium

    ½ = e−t/τ, so t = τ ln 2 ≈ 0.693τ.

  4. A membrane patch has Rm = 100 MΩ and Cm = 100 pF. Find τ. easy

    τ = 108 × 10−10 = 10 ms.

  5. Myelin adds layers of insulation around an axon. Does this increase or decrease the membrane capacitance? Why does that speed up the signal? medium

    Many layers make the insulator much thicker (bigger d), so C = κε0A/d decreases. Less charge is needed to change the voltage, so the membrane charges faster and the signal jumps quickly between the gaps (nodes of Ranvier).

Class 17

Series and parallel circuits; Kirchhoff's laws

By the end you can: combine resistors in series and parallel; apply the junction and loop rules; solve simple circuits.

Combining resistors
Series: R = R1 + R2 + …    Parallel: 1R = 1R1 + 1R2 + …

In series the same current goes through each resistor and the voltages add. In parallel each resistor has the same voltage and the currents add. A parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest resistor.

Kirchhoff's laws
  • Junction (current) rule: the total current into a junction equals the total current out (ΣI = 0). Charge is conserved.
  • Loop (voltage) rule: around any closed loop, the voltage rises equal the voltage drops (ΣV = 0). Energy is conserved.
Simulation

Series or parallel?

Try: switch between series and parallel, and change the two resistors. Check that the numbers obey Kirchhoff's laws.

What to notice: in series, V1 + V2 = battery voltage (loop rule). In parallel, I1 + I2 = total current (junction rule), and the smaller resistor takes the bigger current.

Why this matters for biology

Ion channels in a membrane are resistors in parallel: opening more of them lowers the total resistance. Blood vessels also branch in parallel (Unit 2), so the same rules apply to flow resistance.

Practice problems

  1. 4 Ω and 6 Ω are in series across 12 V. Find the current and the voltage across each. easy

    R = 10 Ω, I = 1.2 A. V1 = 4.8 V, V2 = 7.2 V (sum 12 V).

  2. The same resistors in parallel across 12 V. Find the total resistance and each current. easy

    R = 4 × 6/10 = 2.4 Ω. I1 = 12/4 = 3 A, I2 = 12/6 = 2 A, total 5 A.

  3. Three currents meet at a junction: 2 A and 3 A flow in. What current flows out? easy

    5 A (junction rule).

  4. A 3 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 6 Ω, across 12 V. Find the total current and the current in each 6 Ω resistor. medium

    Parallel pair = 3 Ω, total = 6 Ω, I = 2 A. The pair has 6 V across it, so each 6 Ω carries 1 A.

  5. 100 identical channels, each 1011 Ω, are open in parallel. Find the total resistance. easy

    R = 1011/100 = 109 Ω.

Class 19

Action potentials, ion channels and the electrical neuron

By the end you can: explain the resting potential; describe the stages of an action potential and which channels open; link the membrane's response to RC behaviour; explain how myelin speeds conduction.

19.1The resting potential

The Na+/K+ pump keeps K+ high inside the cell and Na+ high outside. At rest the membrane is mostly permeable to K+, which leaks out and leaves the inside negative: the resting potential is about −70 mV. The membrane is a charged capacitor.

19.2The action potential, step by step

  1. Stimulus: a small current charges the membrane capacitor (RC rise). If it stays below threshold (about −55 mV), the voltage just relaxes back.
  2. Depolarisation: at threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels open. Na+ rushes in and the voltage shoots up to about +30 mV. All or nothing: a bigger stimulus does not make a bigger spike.
  3. Repolarisation: Na+ channels close; voltage-gated K+ channels open. K+ flows out and the voltage falls.
  4. Hyperpolarisation: K+ channels close slowly, so the voltage briefly dips below −70 mV.
  5. Refractory period: for 1–2 ms the cell cannot fire again, so signals travel one way only.
Simulation

Fire a neuron

Try: set a weak stimulus and press Stimulate. Then increase the stimulus until the neuron fires. Then try a very strong stimulus.

What to notice: below threshold the membrane just charges and relaxes like an RC circuit. Once it crosses −55 mV, the spike is always the same height (all or nothing). A stronger stimulus reaches threshold sooner but does not make a bigger spike.

19.3Electrical properties of neurons and the RC link

Key concept

Each patch of axon is an RC circuit: membrane capacitance Cm, membrane (leak) resistance Rm, and the axoplasm's resistance along the axon. The time constant τ = RmCm sets how fast the membrane can follow a stimulus, and the ratio of membrane to axoplasm resistance sets how far a signal spreads passively before fading.

Myelin and conduction speed

Myelin wraps the axon in many insulating layers: Rm goes up (less leak) and Cm goes down (thicker insulator). The signal spreads quickly and far under the myelin and is regenerated only at the gaps (nodes of Ranvier): saltatory conduction. Myelinated fibres conduct at up to about 100 m/s; thin unmyelinated fibres at about 1 m/s. Diseases that destroy myelin, like multiple sclerosis, slow or block nerve signals.

Practice problems

  1. The membrane goes from −70 mV to +30 mV during a spike. What is the change in voltage? easy

    ΔV = 30 − (−70) = 100 mV.

  2. Which ions move, and in which direction, during (a) depolarisation, (b) repolarisation? easy

    (a) Na+ flows in. (b) K+ flows out.

  3. A membrane with τ = 10 ms receives a constant stimulus that would raise it by 20 mV if held forever. How much has it risen after 10 ms? Does it reach a threshold 15 mV above rest? medium

    ΔV = 20(1 − e−1) ≈ 12.6 mV. Not yet; it needs t = −τ ln(1 − 15/20) ≈ 13.9 ms.

  4. A nerve impulse travels 1 m from spinal cord to foot. How long does it take at 100 m/s (myelinated) and at 1 m/s (unmyelinated)? easy

    10 ms and 1 s.

  5. How many Na+ ions must cross 1 µm² of membrane (C = 0.01 F/m²) to change its voltage by 100 mV? medium

    Q = CAΔV = 0.01 × 10−12 × 0.1 = 10−15 C. n = Q/e ≈ 6250 ions, a tiny fraction of the ions in the cell.
