Fluids in Living Systems

Blood, air, lymph and cytoplasm are all fluids. A handful of ideas explains why blood pressure is higher in your feet, why a narrowed artery is so dangerous, and why proteins diffuse slowly.

Class 6

Nature and types of fluids; density, pressure and viscosity

By the end you can: classify fluids; use ρ = m/V and P = F/A with correct units and dimensions; explain what viscosity means.

6.1What is a fluid?

A fluid is anything that flows: it cannot keep its own shape and takes the shape of its container. Liquids and gases are both fluids.

TypeMeaningBiological example
Liquid vs gasLiquids are nearly incompressible; gases compress easilyBlood (liquid), air in lungs (gas)
Ideal vs realIdeal: no viscosity, incompressible. Real fluids have frictionBernoulli assumes ideal; blood is real
Newtonian vs non-NewtonianNewtonian: viscosity stays fixed. Non-Newtonian: viscosity changes with flow speedWater and plasma (Newtonian); whole blood and mucus (non-Newtonian)
Laminar vs turbulent flowSmooth layers vs chaotic swirlsMost vessels (laminar); aorta at peak flow (near turbulent)

6.2Density and pressure

Key concept
ρ = mV     P = FA

Density in kg/m³ (water 1000, blood about 1060). Pressure in pascals, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Doctors measure blood pressure in mmHg: 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa, and 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg.

6.3Dimensions and units

Every quantity can be written in terms of mass [M], length [L] and time [T]. Checking dimensions is the quickest way to catch a wrong formula: both sides must match.

QuantitySI unitDimensions
Density ρkg/m³[M L−3]
Pressure PPa = N/m²[M L−1 T−2]
Viscosity ηPa·s[M L−1 T−1]
Flow rate Qm³/s[L3 T−1]
Diffusion constant Dm²/s[L2 T−1]

6.4Viscosity: fluid friction

Key concept
F = η A ΔvΔy

Imagine fluid between two plates. Drag the top plate and the fluid moves in layers: the layer touching the top plate moves with it, the layer touching the bottom stays still. The force needed depends on the plate area A, the speed gradient Δv/Δy and the viscosity η ("thickness" of the fluid). Water: 0.001 Pa·s. Blood: about 0.003–0.004 Pa·s. Honey: about 10 Pa·s.

Simulation

Slide a plate over a fluid

Try: pick a fluid, then change how fast the top plate moves. Plate area 0.01 m², gap 5 mm.

What to notice: each layer moves a little faster than the one below (arrows grow steadily upward). Honey needs 10,000 times more force than water at the same speed: that is what a large η means.

Why this matters for biology

Pressure differences, not pressure itself, push fluids: air into your lungs and blood through your arteries. Viscosity is what the heart must fight to keep blood moving, and dehydration (which thickens blood) makes that harder.

Practice problems

  1. A blood sample has mass 10.6 g and volume 10 mL. Find its density in kg/m³. easy

    10 mL = 1.0 × 10−5 m³, 10.6 g = 0.0106 kg. ρ = 0.0106/10−5 = 1060 kg/m³.

  2. A force of 50 N acts on 0.010 m². Find the pressure. easy

    P = F/A = 50/0.010 = 5.0 × 103 Pa.

  3. Convert a blood pressure of 120 mmHg into pascals. easy

    120 × 133.3 = 1.60 × 104 Pa (16 kPa).

  4. Use dimensions to find the unit of η from F = ηA(Δv/Δy). medium

    η = FΔy/(AΔv): [M L T−2][L]/([L2][L T−1]) = [M L−1 T−1], i.e. kg/(m·s) = Pa·s.

  5. A plate of area 0.02 m² slides at 0.1 m/s over a 2 mm layer of blood (η = 0.004 Pa·s). Find the force. easy

    F = ηA(v/d) = 0.004 × 0.02 × 0.1/0.002 = 4.0 × 10−3 N.

Class 7

Pascal's law, hydrostatic pressure and buoyancy

By the end you can: use P = P0 + ρgh; apply Pascal's law to a hydraulic lift; use Archimedes' principle to decide float or sink.

7.1Pressure increases with depth

Key concept
P = P0 + ρ g h

The deeper you go, the more fluid sits above you, so the pressure is higher. Only the depth h matters, not the shape of the container.

Where it comes from

Take a column of fluid, area A, height h. The upward push from below (PA) must hold up the push from above (P0A) plus the column's weight mg = ρ(Ah)g. So PA = P0A + ρAhg. Divide by A: P = P0 + ρgh.

Simulation

How high should the IV bag hang?

Try: raise and lower the IV bag. The fluid only flows into the vein if its pressure beats the vein's pressure (about 8 mmHg here).

What to notice: pressure at the needle is ρgh, so doubling the height doubles the pressure and speeds up the drip. Below about 11 cm the bag cannot push fluid into the vein at all.

Worked example · feet vs head

Standing, the blood column from head to feet is about 1.3 m (ρ = 1060 kg/m³). Extra pressure at the feet: ΔP = ρgh = 1060 × 9.8 × 1.3 ≈ 13.5 kPa ≈ 100 mmHg. Leg veins need one-way valves to stop blood pooling.

7.2Pascal's law

Key concept

A pressure change applied to a confined fluid is passed on equally to every part of the fluid. In a hydraulic lift, the same pressure acts on a small and a large piston:

F1A1 = F2A2  ⇒  F2 = F1 A2A1
Simulation

Hydraulic lift (like a hospital bed or dentist's chair)

Try: push on the small piston, then make the big piston wider.

What to notice: the pressure is the same under both pistons, so the force is multiplied by A2/A1. There is no free lunch: the big piston rises a much smaller distance.

Why this matters for biology

Pascal's law is why squeezing one part of a closed fluid system is felt everywhere: pressure in the eye (glaucoma), in the skull (cerebrospinal fluid) and in a blood-pressure cuff is spread through the whole fluid.

7.3Buoyancy: Archimedes' principle

Key concept
Fb = ρfluid g Vdisplaced

A body in a fluid is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of fluid it pushes aside. If the object is less dense than the fluid it floats, with the fraction ρobject/ρfluid under the surface. If it is denser, it sinks.

Simulation

Float or sink?

Try: change the density of the 1-litre block and switch between fluids.

What to notice: a block with half the fluid's density floats half under. Exactly equal densities means it hovers anywhere (neutral buoyancy, like a fish with a tuned swim bladder).

Practice problems

  1. An IV bag is 1.5 m above a vein. Estimate the pressure due to the height (ρ = 1000 kg/m³). easy

    ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 1.47 × 104 Pa ≈ 110 mmHg.

  2. A hydraulic lift has A1 = 0.010 m² and A2 = 0.50 m². If F1 = 120 N, find F2. easy

    F2 = 120 × 0.50/0.010 = 6.0 × 103 N.

  3. A closed container's fluid is pushed by an extra 250 Pa at one point. What is the pressure change elsewhere in the fluid? easy

    250 Pa everywhere (Pascal's law).

  4. A bead of volume 3.0 cm³ is fully under water. Find the buoyant force. easy

    Fb = 1000 × 9.8 × 3.0 × 10−6 = 2.94 × 10−2 N.

  5. An object of density 1050 kg/m³ is placed in water. Does it float or sink? easy

    It sinks, because it is denser than water (1050 > 1000 kg/m³).

  6. Ice (917 kg/m³) floats in sea water (1025 kg/m³). What fraction is under the surface? medium

    Fraction = 917/1025 ≈ 0.89, so about 89% is hidden.

Class 8

Continuity, Bernoulli, laminar vs turbulent flow, Poiseuille's law

By the end you can: use A1v1 = A2v2; use Bernoulli to find pressure changes; decide laminar or turbulent from the Reynolds number; use Poiseuille's law and explain why radius matters so much.

8.1The continuity equation

Key concept
Q = A1v1 = A2v2

Fluid cannot pile up or disappear inside a pipe, so the volume flow rate Q (m³/s) is the same everywhere. Where the pipe is narrow, the fluid must move faster.

8.2Bernoulli's principle

Key concept
P + ½ρv2 + ρgh = constant

Along a streamline of an ideal fluid, pressure energy, kinetic energy and height energy (all per unit volume) trade with each other. At the same height: faster flow means lower pressure.

Simulation

A narrowed artery (stenosis)

Try: make the narrow section narrower. Watch the particle speed and the height of fluid in the two pressure tubes.

What to notice: continuity makes the fluid speed up in the narrow part, and Bernoulli says its pressure drops there. This local low pressure is one reason a stenosis is dangerous: the vessel wall can be pulled inward.

Worked example · Venturi meter

Water flows from A1 = 5.0 cm² to A2 = 1.0 cm² with v1 = 0.50 m/s.

Step 1Continuity: v2 = (A1/A2)v1 = 5 × 0.50 = 2.5 m/s

Step 2Bernoulli (same height): P1 − P2 = ½ρ(v2² − v1²) = ½(1000)(6.25 − 0.25) = 3.0 × 103 Pa

8.3Laminar vs turbulent flow

Key concept
Re = ρ v Dη

The Reynolds number (no units) compares the fluid's tendency to keep moving (inertia) with its friction (viscosity). Re < 2000: laminar, smooth layers. 2000–4000: transitional. Re > 4000: turbulent, swirling.

Simulation

Laminar or turbulent blood flow?

Try: increase the blood speed and the vessel diameter until the dye lines start to swirl.

What to notice: small, slow vessels stay laminar. A wide vessel with fast flow (the aorta at peak heartbeat) gets close to turbulence. Turbulence is noisy: that is what a doctor hears as a murmur or in a blood-pressure cuff.

Blood velocity in the body

Each capillary is tiny, but there are billions of them, so their total area is about 1000 times the aorta's. By continuity, blood slows from about 30 cm/s in the aorta to under 1 mm/s in capillaries, giving oxygen time to diffuse out.

8.4Poiseuille's law: viscous flow in a tube

Real fluids stick to the walls (no-slip). So flow in a tube is fastest in the middle and zero at the wall. The speed profile is a parabola:

Key concept
v(r) = ΔP4ηL(R2 − r2)    Q = πR4ΔP8ηL

R = tube radius, L = length, ΔP = pressure drop along it, η = viscosity. Flow rate goes as R to the fourth power. Halve the radius and flow drops to 1/16.

Derivation in short

Step 1Take an inner cylinder of fluid, radius r. Pressure pushes it forward with ΔP·πr².

Step 2Viscous drag on its surface (area 2πrL) holds it back: −η(dv/dr)(2πrL).

Step 3Steady flow: the two balance, so dv/dr = −ΔP r/(2ηL). Integrate and use v(R) = 0 to get v(r) above.

Step 4Add up the flow through thin rings, dQ = v(r)·2πr dr, from 0 to R: Q = πR4ΔP/(8ηL).

Written as ΔP = Q × (8ηL/πR4), this looks exactly like Ohm's law V = IR (Unit 4). The term 8ηL/πR4 is the flow resistance.

Simulation

Narrow the vessel, watch the flow collapse

Try: shrink the vessel radius to 80%, then 50%. The pressure drop stays fixed.

What to notice: the velocity profile is a parabola, zero at the wall. A 20% narrower vessel keeps only 41% of the flow (0.84). This is why plaque in an artery matters far more than its size suggests.

3D simulation

Inside a blood vessel

Try: drag to look along the vessel or from the side. Then narrow the vessel.

What to notice: cells near the centre (bright yellow) race ahead; cells near the wall (dark red) barely move. The arrows trace the parabola v(r) = (ΔP/4ηL)(R² − r²).

Practice problems

  1. A pipe narrows from 3.0 cm² to 1.2 cm². If v1 = 0.60 m/s, find v2. easy

    v2 = (3.0/1.2) × 0.60 = 1.5 m/s.

  2. In a horizontal artery v1 = 0.4 m/s, P1 = 100 mmHg and v2 = 1.2 m/s. Find P2 (ρ = 1060 kg/m³). medium

    P1 − P2 = ½(1060)(1.44 − 0.16) = 678 Pa = 5.1 mmHg. So P2 ≈ 94.9 mmHg.

  3. Find Re for blood (ρ = 1060 kg/m³, η = 0.004 Pa·s) at 0.50 m/s in a 4.0 mm vessel. Laminar or turbulent? easy

    Re = 1060 × 0.50 × 0.004/0.004 = 530: laminar.

  4. A vessel has R = 2.0 mm, L = 0.20 m, η = 0.004 Pa·s and ΔP = 1200 Pa. Find Q and the centre-line speed. medium

    Q = π(0.002)4(1200)/(8 × 0.004 × 0.20) ≈ 9.42 × 10−6 m³/s (9.4 mL/s). vmax = ΔPR²/(4ηL) = 1200 × 4 × 10−6/0.0032 = 1.5 m/s.

  5. A stenosis reduces a radius to 0.7R with ΔP fixed. By what factor does Q change? easy

    Q ∝ R4: 0.74 = 0.24. Flow drops by about 76%.

  6. A main artery (3.0 cm²) branches into 6 identical arteries whose total area equals the parent's. If v1 = 0.30 m/s, find the speed in each branch. medium

    Total area is unchanged, so the speed is unchanged: 0.30 m/s.

Class 9

Stokes' law, measuring viscosity and Einstein's diffusion relation

By the end you can: find the drag on a small sphere; use a falling-ball viscometer; use D = kBT/6πηr; explain why small molecules diffuse fast and big ones slowly.

9.1Stokes' law: drag on a sphere

Key concept
Fd = 6πη r v

A small sphere of radius r moving slowly at speed v through a fluid of viscosity η feels this drag force. It is valid for slow, laminar motion: red blood cells, bacteria, nanoparticles, dust in air.

9.2Measuring viscosity: the falling-ball viscometer

Drop a ball into a liquid. It speeds up until the drag (plus buoyancy) balances its weight. After that it falls at a constant terminal speed:

Terminal speed
vt = 2 r2 (ρs − ρf) g9 η  ⇒  η = 2 r2 (ρs − ρf) g9 vt

From weight = drag + buoyancy: (4/3)πr³ρsg = 6πηrvt + (4/3)πr³ρfg. Time the ball over a known distance, get vt, and you have η.

Simulation

Falling-ball viscometer

Try: drop a steel ball into different liquids, and change the ball's radius. Then press Drop again.

What to notice: thicker liquids give slower falls (vt ∝ 1/η). Doubling the ball radius makes it fall four times faster (vt ∝ r²). The same physics sets how fast cells settle in a test tube.

9.3Diffusion and the Einstein (Stokes–Einstein) relation

Molecules in a fluid are constantly kicked around by thermal motion. A drop of dye spreads out even in perfectly still water. This random spreading is diffusion, measured by the diffusion constant D (m²/s).

Key concept
D = kBT6πη r    xrms = √2Dt  ⇒  t ≈ x22D

kB = 1.38 × 10−23 J/K (Boltzmann's constant). Hotter means faster diffusion; bigger particles and thicker fluids mean slower diffusion. Distance grows only as √t: to go 10 times farther takes 100 times longer.

Einstein's idea in words

Diffusion (random kicks) and drag (friction) look unrelated, but both come from the same collisions with fluid molecules. Einstein showed that D equals the particle's mobility (1/6πηr, from Stokes' law) times the thermal energy kBT. So friction and randomness are tied together by temperature.

Simulation

Small molecule vs big protein

Try: watch the two clouds spread from the centre. Change the size of the big particle and the temperature. Press Restart to begin again.

What to notice: oxygen-sized particles (green) spread much faster than protein-sized ones (purple). D ∝ 1/r: a particle 25 times bigger diffuses 25 times more slowly. This is why cells use active transport for big cargo.

Worked example · oxygen vs albumin

At 298 K in water (η = 0.001 Pa·s): O2 (r = 0.14 nm) has D ≈ 1.6 × 10−9 m²/s. Albumin (r = 3.5 nm) has D ≈ 6.2 × 10−11 m²/s, about 25 times smaller.

Applications: blood flow and filtration

Diffusion carries O2 and CO2 across the thin capillary wall in milliseconds. Kidney filtration and dialysis membranes let small molecules (urea, glucose) through quickly while holding back big proteins, which diffuse slowly and are too large for the pores.

Practice problems

  1. A sphere of radius 1.0 µm moves at 0.010 m/s in a fluid with η = 0.001 Pa·s. Find the Stokes drag. easy

    F = 6π(0.001)(1.0 × 10−6)(0.010) ≈ 1.88 × 10−10 N.

  2. If the particle radius doubles (all else fixed), by what factor does the Stokes drag change? easy

    F ∝ r, so it doubles.

  3. A red blood cell (r = 3.5 µm) moves at 0.5 mm/s in plasma (η = 0.0015 Pa·s). Find the drag. If η rises by 30%, how does the drag change? medium

    F = 6π(0.0015)(3.5 × 10−6)(5 × 10−4) ≈ 4.9 × 10−11 N. Drag ∝ η, so it rises by 30%.

  4. A steel ball (r = 1.0 mm, ρ = 7800 kg/m³) falls through glycerol (ρ = 1260 kg/m³) at a steady 1.0 cm/s. Find η of glycerol. medium

    η = 2r²(ρs − ρf)g/(9v) = 2(10−6)(6540)(9.8)/(9 × 0.010) ≈ 1.4 Pa·s.

  5. Estimate D for glucose (r = 0.5 nm) in water at 310 K, η = 0.001 Pa·s. easy

    D = (1.38 × 10−23)(310)/(6π × 0.001 × 0.5 × 10−9) ≈ 4.5 × 10−10 m²/s.

  6. O2 (D = 2.0 × 10−9 m²/s) crosses a 5 µm capillary wall. Estimate the time, t ≈ d²/2D. What if the wall is twice as thick? medium

    t = (5 × 10−6)²/(4 × 10−9) ≈ 6.25 ms. Doubling d makes it 4 times longer (25 ms).
