Imaging: Microscopes to Lasers

Lenses let us see cells; the wave nature of light limits how small we can see; and X-rays, diffraction, lasers and holograms open up structures far smaller than a wavelength of light.

Class 23

Microscopes and telescopes: magnification and resolution

By the end you can: use the lens equation; find magnification; explain how a compound microscope and a telescope work; use the resolution limit; describe modern bio-microscopy.

23.1Lenses and images

Key concept
1f = 1do + 1di    m = −dido    P = 1f (dioptres, f in m)

A converging (convex) lens bends parallel rays to a focal point a distance f away. do = object distance, di = image distance. A positive di means a real image on the far side (can be put on a screen, upside down); a negative di means a virtual, upright image (what you see through a magnifying glass). The eye itself is a lens with f ≈ 1.7 cm.

Simulation

Where does the image form?

Try: move the object (the green arrow) toward the lens. Watch what happens as it passes 2f and then f.

What to notice: beyond 2f the image is smaller and inverted (a camera, the eye). Between f and 2f it is bigger and inverted (a microscope objective, a projector). Inside f it becomes upright, virtual and magnified (a magnifying glass, a microscope eyepiece).

23.2The compound microscope

Key concept
M = mobj × Meye ≈ Lfobj × 25 cmfeye

The objective (short focal length) makes a large, real, inverted image inside the tube. The eyepiece acts as a magnifying glass on that image. L is the tube length (about 16 cm) and 25 cm is the closest comfortable viewing distance. A "40×" objective with a "10×" eyepiece gives 400×.

23.3The telescope

A telescope looks at distant objects. Its objective has a long focal length and a large diameter to collect light; the eyepiece magnifies the image. Angular magnification M = −fobj/feye. Biologists use the same idea in binoculars for field work and in the long-working-distance lenses of surgical microscopes.

3D simulation

A lens bends a whole bundle of rays

Try: drag to rotate. Change the lens's focal length and the beam width.

What to notice: every ray parallel to the axis passes through the same focal point, from all around the lens, not just in one plane. A stronger lens (shorter f) bends the rays more sharply; a wider beam makes a steeper cone, which is what a high-NA objective does.

23.4Resolution: why magnification is not enough

Key concept
dmin = 0.61 λNA    NA = n sin α    (telescope or eye: θmin = 1.22 λ/D)

Light spreads out (diffracts) as it passes through a lens, so each point of the object becomes a small blurry disc. Two points closer than dmin blur into one. Resolution is the smallest separation you can still tell apart. It improves with shorter wavelength and larger numerical aperture NA (n = refractive index between lens and sample, α = half-angle of the cone of light the lens collects). Oil immersion (n = 1.5) pushes NA to about 1.4, giving dmin ≈ 0.2 µm. Magnifying more than this allows is "empty magnification".

Simulation

Can you tell two dots apart?

Try: two tiny fluorescent beads sit 300 nm apart. Change the light colour and the objective's NA until you can see two separate peaks.

What to notice: blue light and a high-NA oil objective resolve the pair; red light and a low-NA objective blur them into one blob. This is why the smallest bacteria (about 0.2 µm) are at the edge of what a light microscope can show, and viruses are not resolved at all.

23.5Modern bio-microscopy

TechniqueIdeaTypical use
Phase contrastTurns tiny differences in how much a cell slows light into brightness differencesLiving, unstained cells
FluorescenceDyes or proteins (e.g. GFP) absorb one colour and emit a longer one; filters show only the emitted lightLabelling particular proteins, DNA, organelles
ConfocalA pinhole blocks out-of-focus light, so you get sharp optical slices and 3D stacksThick tissues, 3D cell structure
Super-resolutionSwitches fluorescent molecules on a few at a time to beat the 0.2 µm limitStructures down to about 20 nm
Electron microscopeUses electrons, whose wavelength is thousands of times shorter than light'sViruses, membranes, single proteins (cryo-EM)

Practice problems

  1. An object is 30 cm from a lens with f = 10 cm. Find the image distance and magnification. Real or virtual? easy

    1/di = 1/10 − 1/30 ⇒ di = 15 cm. m = −15/30 = −0.5: real, inverted, half size.

  2. The object moves to 5 cm from the same lens. Find di and m. easy

    1/di = 1/10 − 1/5 ⇒ di = −10 cm (virtual). m = −(−10)/5 = +2: upright, twice as big (magnifying glass).

  3. A microscope has a 4 mm objective, a 25 mm eyepiece and a 160 mm tube. Find the total magnification. easy

    M = (160/4) × (250/25) = 40 × 10 = 400×.

  4. Find dmin for green light (550 nm) with (a) an NA = 1.4 oil objective, (b) an NA = 0.25 objective. easy

    (a) 0.61 × 550/1.4 ≈ 240 nm. (b) 0.61 × 550/0.25 ≈ 1.34 µm.

  5. A lens has f = 50 cm. What is its power? easy

    P = 1/0.5 m = +2 dioptres.

  6. A telescope has fobj = 100 cm and feye = 2.5 cm. Find its magnification. easy

    |M| = 100/2.5 = 40×.

Class 24

Interference, diffraction, X-rays, lasers and holography

By the end you can: use the double-slit and grating equations; explain X-ray production and imaging; use Bragg's law; describe what makes laser light special; explain the idea of a hologram.

24.1Interference

Key concept

When two waves overlap they add. If crest meets crest they reinforce (constructive interference); if crest meets trough they cancel (destructive). It depends on the path difference: a whole number of wavelengths gives bright, a half-number gives dark.

Young's double slit: d sin θ = mλ    fringe spacing: Δy = λ Ld

d = slit separation, L = distance to the screen, m = 0, 1, 2, …

Simulation

Young's double slit

Try: change the wavelength (colour) and the slit separation. Watch the fringes on the screen.

What to notice: red light (long λ) gives wider fringes than blue. Moving the slits closer together spreads the fringes out. Light behaving like this is the proof that it is a wave.

24.2Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave through a gap or around an edge. It matters when the gap is not much bigger than the wavelength. A single slit of width a gives a central bright band with dark fringes at a sinθ = mλ. A diffraction grating (many slits, spacing d) gives very sharp bright lines at d sinθ = mλ; spectrometers use gratings to split light into colours to identify molecules.

24.3X-rays

Production and imaging

In an X-ray tube, electrons are accelerated through a high voltage V and smash into a metal target (usually tungsten). Their sudden stop gives a continuous spread of X-rays (bremsstrahlung) plus sharp lines from the target atoms. The shortest wavelength comes when an electron gives all its energy eV to one photon:

λmin = hceV    I = I0 e−μx

X-rays fade exponentially through matter; μ (attenuation coefficient) is much larger for bone (calcium) than for soft tissue, so bone casts a shadow. CT takes many X-ray images around the body and computes cross-sections.

Simulation

X-ray image of an arm

Try: change the tube voltage. Higher kV means more penetrating X-rays (smaller μ).

What to notice: bone lets much less through than soft tissue, so it shows up white on the film. At very high kV everything gets through and the contrast falls: radiographers choose the kV to get the best contrast for each body part.

24.4X-ray diffraction: seeing molecules

Bragg's law
2 d sin θ = n λ

X-ray wavelengths (about 0.1 nm) are similar to the spacing d between atoms in a crystal. Rays reflected from neighbouring layers of atoms interfere; they add up strongly only at angles where the extra path 2d sinθ is a whole number of wavelengths. Measuring these angles reveals the spacing and arrangement of atoms. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray pattern of DNA revealed its double helix; today X-ray crystallography solves protein structures for drug design.

Simulation

Bragg reflection from a crystal

Try: sweep the angle θ and watch the detector. Then change the layer spacing d.

What to notice: the two reflected rays are in step (bright) only when 2d sinθ = nλ. At other angles they partly cancel. A bigger spacing gives bright reflections at smaller angles.

24.5Lasers

Key concept

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Atoms are "pumped" into an excited state (population inversion). A passing photon can stimulate an excited atom to emit an identical photon: same wavelength, same direction, same phase. Mirrors bounce the light back and forth so it builds up. Laser light is:

  • Monochromatic: one wavelength;
  • Coherent: all waves in step;
  • Directional: a narrow beam that hardly spreads;
  • Intense: can be focused to a tiny, very bright spot.

Uses in biology and medicine: eye surgery (LASIK, retinal repair), cutting and sealing tissue, removing tattoos and birthmarks, confocal and super-resolution microscopy, flow cytometry, DNA sequencers, optical tweezers that hold single cells.

Simulation

Lamp light vs laser light

Try: switch between an ordinary lamp and a laser.

What to notice: the lamp's waves have many colours, many directions and random phases, so they add up messily. The laser's waves are one colour, one direction and all in step, so they add up to a strong, clean beam.

24.6Holography

An ordinary photo records only how bright the light is. A hologram records the light's phase too. A laser beam is split in two: the reference beam goes straight to the film, the object beam reflects off the object first. Where they meet they make an interference pattern, which the film records. Shine the reference beam on the developed film and it diffracts the light to rebuild the original wavefront, so you see a true 3D image that changes as you move your head. Laser coherence is essential, otherwise there is no stable interference pattern. Uses: security marks on cards and banknotes, holographic data storage, and digital holographic microscopy, which measures the thickness and volume of living cells without staining.

Practice problems

  1. Light of 600 nm passes two slits 0.2 mm apart onto a screen 1 m away. Find the fringe spacing. easy

    Δy = λL/d = 600 × 10−9 × 1/0.2 × 10−3 = 3.0 mm.

  2. A grating has 600 lines per mm. At what angle is the first-order line of 500 nm light? medium

    d = 1/600 mm = 1.67 µm. sinθ = λ/d = 0.30, θ ≈ 17.5°.

  3. X-rays of 0.154 nm reflect from atomic layers 0.20 nm apart. Find the first-order Bragg angle. medium

    sinθ = λ/(2d) = 0.154/0.40 = 0.385, θ ≈ 22.6°.

  4. Find the shortest X-ray wavelength from a 30 kV tube. easy

    λmin = hc/(eV) = (6.63 × 10−34 × 3 × 108)/(1.6 × 10−19 × 3 × 104) ≈ 0.041 nm.

  5. Bone has μ = 0.5 cm−1. What fraction of X-rays passes through 2 cm? What thickness halves the beam? medium

    I/I0 = e−1 ≈ 0.37. Half-value thickness = ln 2/μ ≈ 1.39 cm.

  6. Give two properties of laser light that make holography possible. easy

    It is coherent (fixed phase, so the interference pattern is stable) and monochromatic (one wavelength, so the pattern is sharp).
