Rotation, Momentum and Energy

Everything you know about moving in a straight line has a twin for spinning. Joints, centrifuges, bacterial flagella and dividing cells all rotate, and the same few rules describe them.

Class 1

Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration

By the end you can: describe spinning with θ, ω, α; link them to linear motion with v = rω; use the four constant-α equations; convert rpm to rad/s.

Big idea

For straight-line motion we use position x, velocity v and acceleration a. For spinning we swap distance for angle. Every straight-line formula you already know has a spinning twin.

1.1Why biotechnologists care

A centrifuge separates blood plasma from cells by spinning tubes thousands of times per minute. A bacterium swims by rotating its flagellum like a propeller. ATP synthase, the enzyme that makes most of your ATP, is a tiny rotating motor. Your elbow, knee and hip are pivots. To understand any of these we need a language for rotation.

1.2The three angular quantities

Key concept
  • Angular displacement θ: how far something has turned, in radians. One full turn = 2π rad = 360°.
  • Angular velocity ω = Δθ/Δt: how fast it turns, in rad/s.
  • Angular acceleration α = Δω/Δt: how fast the spin rate changes, in rad/s².
QuantityLinearAngularLink (point at radius r)
Displacementx (m)θ (rad)s = rθ (arc length)
Velocityv (m/s)ω (rad/s)v = rω
Accelerationa (m/s²)α (rad/s²)at = rα

On a merry-go-round everyone completes a turn in the same time, so everyone has the same ω. But a child at the edge travels a bigger circle, so has a larger v. The simulation below shows exactly this.

Simulation

Two ladybugs on a turntable

Try: change the spin rate, then move ladybug B outward. Watch the two speed arrows.

What to notice: both bugs always share the same ω (they turn together), but the bug farther out moves faster, because v = rω. Double r and the speed doubles.

1.3Equations for constant angular acceleration

These are the straight-line equations with x→θ, v→ω, a→α. Use them only when α is constant.

Rotational kinematics
ω = ω0 + αt    θ = ω0t + ½αt2
ω2 = ω02 + 2αθ    θ = ½(ω0 + ω)t

Unit conversions: 1 revolution = 2π rad  ·  ω (rad/s) = rpm × 2π/60  ·  revolutions = θ/2π

Simulation

Spinning up a centrifuge rotor

Try: set a starting speed ω0 and an angular acceleration α, then press Start. Pause at any time and check the numbers with the equations above.

What to notice: the ω–t graph is a straight line whose slope is α. The rotor turns through more and more angle each second, because it keeps getting faster. Set α negative to see it slow down.

Worked example 1 · from rest

A drill chuck starts from rest with α = 4.0 rad/s². Find ω after 3.0 s and the angle turned.

Step 1ω = ω0 + αt = 0 + (4.0)(3.0) = 12 rad/s

Step 2θ = ½αt² = ½(4.0)(3.0)² = 18 rad ≈ 18/2π = 2.9 revolutions

Worked example 2 · with rpm

A blender blade speeds up from 600 rpm to 1800 rpm in 4.0 s. Find α and the number of revolutions.

Step 1Convert: ω0 = 600 × 2π/60 = 20π rad/s; ω = 1800 × 2π/60 = 60π rad/s

Step 2α = (ω − ω0)/t = 40π/4.0 = 10π ≈ 31.4 rad/s²

Step 3θ = ½(ω0 + ω)t = ½(80π)(4.0) = 160π rad = 80 revolutions

Practice problems

  1. A wheel starts from rest with constant α = 2.5 rad/s². Find (a) ω after 6 s, (b) the angle turned. easy

    (a) ω = 0 + 2.5 × 6 = 15 rad/s. (b) θ = ½ × 2.5 × 6² = 45 rad.

  2. A microscope stage speeds up from 10 rad/s to 25 rad/s in 3 s. Find (a) α, (b) the angular displacement. easy

    (a) α = (25 − 10)/3 = 5 rad/s². (b) θ = ½(10 + 25)(3) = 52.5 rad.

  3. A rotor completes 5 revolutions in 2 s. Find ω in rad/s. easy

    θ = 5 × 2π = 10π rad, so ω = 10π/2 = 5π ≈ 15.7 rad/s.

  4. A ceiling fan goes from 0 to 300 rpm in 20 s. Find (a) α, (b) the number of revolutions. easy

    ω = 300 × 2π/60 = 10π rad/s. (a) α = 10π/20 = π/2 ≈ 1.57 rad/s². (b) θ = ½(0 + 10π)(20) = 100π rad = 50 revolutions.

  5. A bicycle wheel of radius 0.35 m spins at 150 rpm. Find (a) ω in rad/s, (b) the speed of a point on the rim. easy

    (a) ω = 150 × 2π/60 = 5π ≈ 15.7 rad/s. (b) v = rω = 0.35 × 15.7 ≈ 5.50 m/s.

  6. A fan slows from 25 rad/s to 5 rad/s in 10 s. Find (a) the angular deceleration, (b) the time it takes to stop completely from 25 rad/s. medium

    (a) α = (5 − 25)/10 = −2 rad/s² (2 rad/s² of deceleration). (b) 0 = 25 − 2t, so t = 12.5 s.

Class 2

Torque, Newton's second law for rotation, and equilibrium

By the end you can: calculate torque; explain why τ = Iα; use Στ = 0 to find muscle forces in a joint.

2.1What is torque?

A force makes things move. A torque makes things turn. Push a door near the hinge and it barely moves; push at the handle and it swings easily. Same force, different effect.

Key concept
τ = r F sin θ

r = distance from the pivot to where the force acts (the lever arm), F = force, θ = angle between the force and the lever arm. Unit: N·m. Torque is biggest when you push at 90° (sin 90° = 1) and zero when you push straight along the arm (sin 0° = 0).

Simulation

Push the door

Try: keep the force the same and move the push point toward the hinge. Then change the angle from 90° to 0°.

What to notice: torque grows with both force and distance from the hinge. Only the part of the force that is perpendicular to the door (F sinθ, the orange arrow) turns it.

2.2Where does τ = Iα come from?

Let's derive it

Step 1Think of a rigid body as many small masses mi, each at distance ri from the axis. Newton's law for one mass: Fi = miai.

Step 2For rotation ai = riα, and every piece shares the same α. So Fi = miriα.

Step 3Multiply by ri to get each torque: τi = miri²α.

Step 4Add them up: τtotal = (Σ miri²) α.

Step 5Call the bracket the moment of inertia I = Σ miri². Result:

τ = Iα

This is the spinning twin of F = ma. Mass resists being pushed; moment of inertia resists being spun.

Worked example · swinging the forearm

Treat forearm plus hand as a 1.2 kg point mass at r = 0.30 m from the elbow. The biceps gives τ = 4.5 N·m. Find α.

Step 1I = mr² = 1.2 × 0.30² = 0.108 kg·m²

Step 2α = τ/I = 4.5/0.108 ≈ 41.7 rad/s²

2.3Equilibrium of rotating bodies

Key concept

A body does not start to rotate when the torques balance: Στ = 0, i.e. clockwise torques = anticlockwise torques. (For full equilibrium the forces must balance too: ΣF = 0.)

Why this matters for biology

The elbow is a third-class lever: pivot (elbow), then effort (biceps tendon, close to the elbow), then load (hand, far away). Because the biceps is so close to the pivot, it must pull many times harder than the weight you hold. You lose force but gain speed and range of motion.

Simulation

Hold the dumbbell steady

Try: increase the dumbbell mass, then move the tendon attachment farther from the elbow. Watch the biceps force.

What to notice: balancing torques gives Fbiceps × d = W × x. With d = 4 cm and x = 32 cm the muscle pulls 8 times the weight. Moving the tendon outward a little makes holding much easier.

Worked example · biceps force

A 2.0 kg dumbbell is held 0.32 m from the elbow, forearm horizontal. The biceps attaches 0.04 m from the elbow and pulls straight up. Ignore the forearm's weight.

Step 1Balance torques about the elbow: Fb(0.04) = (2.0)(9.8)(0.32)

Step 2Fb = 6.272/0.04 = 157 N, about 8 times the 19.6 N weight.

Practice problems

  1. A 15 N force acts at 60° to a lever arm 0.5 m long. Find the torque. easy

    τ = rF sinθ = 0.5 × 15 × sin 60° = 6.50 N·m.

  2. A bar 0.8 m long is pivoted at one end. A 12 N force acts at the free end at 30° to the bar. Find the torque. easy

    τ = 0.8 × 12 × sin 30° = 4.8 N·m.

  3. A forearm holds a 25 N load 0.30 m from the elbow. The biceps attaches 0.05 m from the elbow and pulls perpendicular to the forearm. Find the biceps force. easy

    Fb(0.05) = 25(0.30), so Fb = 150 N.

  4. A wheel has I = 0.75 kg·m². What torque gives it α = 4 rad/s²? easy

    τ = Iα = 0.75 × 4 = 3.0 N·m.

  5. A forearm (0.35 m long, mass 2.2 kg, weight acting at its middle) holds a 4.0 kg load at the hand (0.35 m). The biceps attaches 0.05 m from the elbow at 70° to the forearm. Find (a) the torque of the load about the elbow, (b) the biceps force for equilibrium. medium

    (a) τload = 4.0 × 9.8 × 0.35 = 13.7 N·m.

    (b) Forearm: 2.2 × 9.8 × 0.175 = 3.77 N·m. Total clockwise = 17.5 N·m. Biceps: F × 0.05 × sin 70° = 17.5, so F ≈ 372 N.

  6. During standing, the ground force (700 N) passes 0.06 m in front of the knee. What torque must the quadriceps supply to stop the knee buckling? easy

    τ = F × d = 700 × 0.06 = 42 N·m.

Class 3

Moment of inertia

By the end you can: calculate I for point masses, a rod and a disc; use the parallel-axis and perpendicular-axis theorems.

3.1Definition

Key concept
I = Σ mi ri2   (unit kg·m²)

I measures how hard it is to spin something. It depends on the mass and on how far that mass sits from the axis, and distance counts squared. Moving mass twice as far out makes it four times harder to spin.

Simulation

Same push, different spin

Try: both rotors get exactly the same torque. Slide the masses on rotor B outward and press Restart.

What to notice: with the masses twice as far out, I is four times bigger and rotor B spins up four times more slowly. This is why a gymnast tucks in to spin fast and why a bent knee swings forward more easily than a straight leg.

3.2Simple bodies

For a solid object we add up (integrate) all the tiny pieces: I = ∫ r² dm. You only need the results:

Body (mass M)AxisI
Point mass at distance rthrough pivotMr²
Thin rod, length Lthrough centre, perpendicularML²/12
Thin rod, length Lthrough one end, perpendicularML²/3
Solid disc or cylinder, radius Rthrough centre, perpendicular to faceMR²/2
Ring or thin hoop, radius Rthrough centre, perpendicular to faceMR²
Solid disc, radius Ralong a diameterMR²/4
Why this matters for biology

A limb turns about a joint at its end, not its middle. A rod about its end has I = ML²/3, four times bigger than about its centre. That is why swinging a straight leg from the hip takes so much effort.

3.3Parallel-axis theorem

Key concept
I = Icm + M d2

If you know I about an axis through the centre of mass, the moment of inertia about any parallel axis a distance d away is bigger by Md². The smallest I is always about the centre of mass.

Check it: rod about its end

Icm = ML²/12, and the end is d = L/2 from the centre. Iend = ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/12 + 3ML²/12 = ML²/3. It matches the table.

Simulation

Move the axis along a rod

Try: drag the slider to move the pivot from the centre of the rod to its end.

What to notice: I is smallest at the centre and grows as d² (the curve is a parabola). At the end, d = L/2 and I becomes ML²/3.

3.4Perpendicular-axis theorem

Key concept (flat objects only)
Iz = Ix + Iy

For a flat plate in the x–y plane, I about the z axis (perpendicular to the plate) equals the sum of the I values about two perpendicular axes lying in the plate. Example: a disc has Iz = MR²/2, and by symmetry Ix = Iy, so each diameter gives MR²/4.

Worked example · forearm as a rod

Model a forearm as a uniform rod, M = 1.5 kg, L = 0.35 m, turning about the elbow (one end). The biceps gives 4.5 N·m.

Step 1I = ML²/3 = 1.5 × 0.35²/3 ≈ 0.0613 kg·m²

Step 2α = τ/I = 4.5/0.0613 ≈ 73.4 rad/s²

Practice problems

  1. Two 0.5 kg masses sit at the ends of a light rod 0.6 m long that turns about its centre. Find I. easy

    Each mass is 0.3 m from the axis: I = 2 × 0.5 × 0.3² = 0.09 kg·m².

  2. A centrifuge rotor is a solid disc, M = 3.0 kg, R = 0.10 m. Find I about its axle. easy

    I = ½MR² = 0.5 × 3.0 × 0.01 = 0.015 kg·m².

  3. A uniform rod (M = 2 kg, L = 1.2 m) turns about one end. Find I. Then use the parallel-axis theorem to check it from Icm. easy

    I = ML²/3 = 2 × 1.44/3 = 0.96 kg·m². Check: Icm = 2 × 1.44/12 = 0.24; Md² = 2 × 0.6² = 0.72; total 0.96 kg·m².

  4. A 1.0 kg rod of length 0.40 m turns about an axis 0.10 m from its centre. Find I. medium

    I = ML²/12 + Md² = 1.0 × 0.16/12 + 1.0 × 0.01 = 0.0133 + 0.0100 = 0.0233 kg·m².

  5. A thin disc has I = 0.02 kg·m² about its central perpendicular axis. What is I about a diameter? easy

    Perpendicular-axis theorem: Iz = Ix + Iy = 2Idiameter, so Idiameter = 0.01 kg·m².

Class 4

Angular momentum, impulse, work and rotational energy

By the end you can: use L = Iω and its conservation; relate torque to change of L; use impulse J = FΔt = Δp; find rotational kinetic energy and work; explain centrifuges and molecular motors.

4.1Linear momentum and impulse

Key concept
p = m v    J = F Δt    Impulse–momentum theorem: F Δt = Δp

Momentum is "mass in motion" (a vector, kg·m/s). Impulse is a force acting for a time (N·s). Start from F = ma = m(Δv/Δt), multiply by Δt, and you get FΔt = mΔv = Δp.

Rearranged, F = Δp / Δt. For the same change in momentum, a longer stopping time means a smaller force. This is the body's favourite trick: bending the knees on landing, cartilage in joints, cushioned shoes.

Simulation

Stiff or soft landing?

Try: keep the mass and speed fixed, then make the landing time longer (bend the knees more).

What to notice: the shaded area (force × time = impulse) stays the same, because Δp is fixed. Stretch the time 8 times and the force drops 8 times.

Worked example · jumping off a wall

A 60 kg person lands at 3.0 m/s and stops. Δp = 60 × 3.0 = 180 kg·m/s.

Stiff legs (Δt = 0.05 s): F = 180/0.05 = 3600 N. Bent knees (Δt = 0.40 s): F = 180/0.40 = 450 N.

4.2Angular momentum

Key concept
L = I ω    τ = ΔLΔt    angular impulse τΔt = ΔL

These are the twins of p = mv and F = Δp/Δt. If no outside torque acts, L stays constant:

I1ω1 = I2ω2
Simulation

The spinning diver

Try: pull the arms in and push them out again while the figure spins.

What to notice: L = Iω never changes. Arms in makes I small, so ω must grow. The kinetic energy goes up too; the muscles did work pulling the arms in.

3D simulation

Angular momentum is a vector

Try: drag the picture to look from any side. Change the spin rate (negative = the other way) and switch between a disc and a ring of the same mass.

What to notice: L points along the axle (right-hand rule: curl your fingers the way it spins, your thumb gives L). The ring has all its mass at the rim, so its I and L are twice the disc's at the same ω.

4.3Work and rotational kinetic energy

Key concept
Krot = ½ I ω2    W = τ θ    W = ΔKrot

Compare with K = ½mv² and W = Fd. A torque acting through an angle does work, and that work changes the rotational kinetic energy (work–energy theorem). Power is P = τω.

4.4Applications: centrifuges and molecular motors

Centrifuge. A sample spinning at radius r has centripetal acceleration a = ω²r. Biologists quote this as relative centrifugal force, RCF = ω²r/g, in "× g". Denser particles (cells, then organelles, then large molecules) are pushed outward and form a pellet.

Simulation

Centrifuge a blood sample

Try: set the speed in rpm and the rotor radius, then watch the red cells settle in the tube.

What to notice: RCF grows with rpm squared. Doubling the rpm makes the cells settle four times faster. Plasma (yellow) stays on top; red cells pack at the bottom.

Molecular motors. The bacterial flagellar motor spins the flagellum at around 100 revolutions per second, driven by ions flowing through the membrane. ATP synthase turns its central shaft in 120° steps, making one ATP per step. Both obey τ = Iα and W = τθ just like a centrifuge, only at the nanometre scale.

Worked example · energy in a rotor

The 0.015 kg·m² rotor spins at 6000 rpm. ω = 6000 × 2π/60 = 628 rad/s. K = ½(0.015)(628)² ≈ 2.96 kJ. That is why a centrifuge lid must stay locked.

Practice problems

  1. A 0.15 kg ball is thrown at 25 m/s. Find its momentum. easy

    p = mv = 0.15 × 25 = 3.75 kg·m/s.

  2. A racket pushes a ball with 40 N for 0.02 s. Find the impulse. easy

    J = FΔt = 40 × 0.02 = 0.8 N·s.

  3. A 0.5 kg ball moving at 8 m/s is hit and leaves at 8 m/s the other way. Contact lasts 0.01 s. Find (a) Δp, (b) the average force. medium

    (a) Δp = m(vf − vi) = 0.5(8 − (−8)) = 8 kg·m/s. (b) F = 8/0.01 = 800 N. The direction reversal doubles Δp.

  4. A 55 kg gymnast lands at 4.5 m/s. Find the average force if the landing takes (a) 0.06 s, (b) 0.35 s. easy

    Δp = 55 × 4.5 = 247.5 kg·m/s. (a) 247.5/0.06 = 4125 N. (b) 247.5/0.35 ≈ 707 N.

  5. A skater spins at 1.5 rev/s with I = 4.0 kg·m², then pulls in the arms to I = 1.6 kg·m². Find the new spin rate. easy

    I1ω1 = I2ω2: ω2 = 4.0 × 1.5/1.6 = 3.75 rev/s.

  6. A constant torque of 2.0 N·m turns a wheel through 10 revolutions. How much work is done? easy

    θ = 10 × 2π = 20π rad. W = τθ = 2.0 × 20π ≈ 126 J.

  7. A rotor spinning at 10,000 rpm holds a tube 8.0 cm from the axis. Find the RCF. medium

    ω = 10,000 × 2π/60 = 1047 rad/s. RCF = ω²r/g = 1047² × 0.08/9.8 ≈ 8950 × g.

  8. A uniform rod (2 kg, 1.2 m) pivoted at one end is released from horizontal. Using energy, find ω when it hangs vertically. medium

    The centre falls L/2 = 0.6 m: Mg(L/2) = ½(ML²/3)ω², so ω = √(3g/L) = √(3 × 9.8/1.2) ≈ 4.95 rad/s.

  9. A centrifuge reaches 30,000 rpm in 20 s from rest. Find (a) α, (b) the number of revolutions while speeding up. medium

    ω = 30,000 × 2π/60 = 1000π rad/s. (a) α = 1000π/20 ≈ 157 rad/s². (b) θ = ½(1000π)(20) = 10,000π rad = 5000 rev.
