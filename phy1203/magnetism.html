Magnetism, EM Waves and MRI

Moving charges make magnetic fields and feel magnetic forces. Changing fields make light, radio and X-rays. And the magnetism of the protons in your body's water is what an MRI scanner listens to.

Class 20

Magnetic fields and the force on moving charges

By the end you can: describe a magnetic field and its sources; use F = qvB sinθ and the right-hand rule; find the radius of a charge's circular path; give examples of magnetism in biology.

20.1Magnetic field

Key concept

A magnetic field B surrounds magnets and moving charges (currents). Field lines leave a magnet's north pole and enter its south pole, and always form closed loops. Unit: tesla (T). Earth's field ≈ 50 µT; a fridge magnet ≈ 5 mT; an MRI scanner 1.5–3 T.

Field around a long straight wire: B = μ0 I2π r   μ0 = 4π × 10−7 T·m/A

20.2Force on a moving charge

Key concept
F = q v B sin θ    on a wire: F = B I L sin θ

θ is the angle between the velocity and the field. The force is perpendicular to both v and B (right-hand rule: fingers along v, curl them toward B, thumb gives F for a positive charge). A charge at rest, or one moving along the field, feels no magnetic force.

Because the force is always sideways, it never speeds the charge up; it only bends its path into a circle:

qvB = mv2r  ⇒  r = m vq B
Simulation

A charge in a magnetic field

Try: the field points into the screen (⊗). Change the speed and the field strength, and switch between a proton and an electron.

What to notice: faster charges make bigger circles (r ∝ v); a stronger field makes tighter circles (r ∝ 1/B). The electron is much lighter, so it circles much more tightly and turns the other way because its charge is negative.

3D simulation

Spiralling along a field line

Try: drag to rotate. Change the angle between the velocity and the field.

What to notice: only the part of v across the field (v sinθ) is bent into a circle; the part along the field (v cosθ) carries on. The result is a helix. At 90° it is a flat circle; at 0° it is a straight line with no force. Charged particles from the Sun spiral along Earth's field this way to make the auroras.

20.3Magnetic effects in biology

  • Magnetotactic bacteria grow chains of tiny magnetite (Fe3O4) crystals that act as a compass needle. The torque from Earth's field lines the cell up, and it swims along the field lines down toward the low-oxygen mud it prefers.
  • Birds, sea turtles and salmon sense Earth's field for navigation, probably with magnetite or with light-sensitive proteins (cryptochromes) in the eye.
  • Nerve and heart currents make extremely weak magnetic fields (the brain's are about 10−13 T). Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures them with ultra-sensitive sensors.
  • MRI uses strong fields to read the magnetism of protons in water (Class 21). Magnetic forces on ions in moving blood also give an electromagnetic blood-flow meter.
Simulation

Magnetotactic bacteria follow the field

Try: rotate the magnetic field direction, and make the field stronger or weaker.

What to notice: each cell's magnetite chain feels a torque τ = μB sinθ that turns it toward the field. In a strong field they line up neatly and swim in a group; in a weak field random jostling wins and they wander.

Practice problems

  1. A proton moves at 106 m/s at right angles to a 1.5 T field. Find the force. easy

    F = qvB = 1.6 × 10−19 × 106 × 1.5 = 2.4 × 10−13 N.

  2. Find the radius of that proton's path (mp = 1.67 × 10−27 kg). medium

    r = mv/(qB) = 1.67 × 10−27 × 106/(1.6 × 10−19 × 1.5) ≈ 7.0 mm.

  3. What is the force on a charge moving parallel to B? easy

    θ = 0, sin 0 = 0, so F = 0.

  4. Find B at 5 cm from a long wire carrying 10 A. Compare with Earth's field. easy

    B = μ0I/(2πr) = 4π × 10−7 × 10/(2π × 0.05) = 4 × 10−5 T, about the same as Earth's field.

  5. A 0.2 m wire carries 2 A at right angles to a 0.5 T field. Find the force. easy

    F = BIL = 0.5 × 2 × 0.2 = 0.2 N.

Class 21

EM oscillations and waves, the EM spectrum, NMR and MRI

By the end you can: explain LC oscillations; describe an EM wave; use c = fλ and E = hf; name the spectrum regions with their properties and uses; compare α, β, γ rays; explain NMR and the basics of MRI.

21.1Electromagnetic oscillations

Key concept
f = 12π√LC

Connect a charged capacitor to a coil (inductor L). The capacitor drives a current through the coil; the coil's magnetic field keeps the current going and charges the capacitor the other way; and so on. Energy sloshes back and forth between the electric field of the capacitor (½CV²) and the magnetic field of the coil (½LI²), like a mass on a spring. Radio transmitters, the RF coils of an MRI and the tuning circuits of a receiver all use LC oscillators.

Simulation

LC oscillator: energy sloshes back and forth

Try: watch the two energy bars. Then change L and C and read the frequency.

What to notice: when the capacitor is full the current is zero, and when the current is biggest the capacitor is empty. The total energy stays constant. Bigger L or C means slower oscillation.

21.2Electromagnetic waves

Key concept
c = f λ = 3.00 × 108 m/s    E = cB    photon energy: E = hf = hcλ

An oscillating charge sends out an electromagnetic wave: an electric field and a magnetic field, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, each making the other. EM waves need no medium and all travel at c in vacuum. h = 6.63 × 10−34 J·s (Planck's constant).

3D simulation

An electromagnetic wave

Try: drag to view it from different sides. Change the wavelength.

What to notice: E (red) wiggles up and down, B (blue) wiggles side to side, and the wave moves along the third direction. E and B rise and fall together. This is a transverse wave.

Try: put paper, aluminium or lead in front of the three beams.

What to notice: paper stops alpha, aluminium stops beta, and even lead only weakens gamma. Charged particles in a magnetic field would bend in opposite directions (Class 20); gamma, having no charge, would not bend at all.

21.5Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)

Key concept
fLarmor = γ2π B    for protons: γ2π = 42.58 MHz/T
  1. A proton (hydrogen nucleus) spins and behaves like a tiny bar magnet.
  2. In a strong field B, the proton magnets line up (slightly more along B than against it) and precess (wobble like a spinning top) at the Larmor frequency.
  3. A pulse of radio waves at exactly that frequency is absorbed (resonance) and tips the protons over.
  4. When the pulse stops, the protons relax back, sending out a radio signal. Two relaxation times: T1 (realigning with B) and T2 (losing step with each other).
3D simulation

Proton spins and resonance

Try: set the magnet strength, then tune the radio frequency. Press RF pulse. Only when the radio frequency matches the Larmor frequency do the spins tip over. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: the spins wobble (precess) faster in a stronger field. Resonance is very sharp: a few percent off and nothing happens. That sharpness is what lets MRI pick out one slice of the body at a time.

21.6MRI basics

The body is mostly water, so it is full of protons. An MRI scanner has a strong main magnet (1.5–3 T), extra gradient coils that make the field slightly different at each place (so each place has its own Larmor frequency, which tells the computer where a signal came from), and RF coils that send pulses and pick up the signal. Tissues differ in water content and in their T1 and T2, which gives contrast between fat, muscle, brain matter and fluid. MRI uses radio waves, not ionising radiation, so it is safe for repeated scans; but metal objects near the magnet are dangerous.

Practice problems

  1. Find the resonance (LC) frequency for L = 1 mH and C = 1 nF. easy

    f = 1/(2π√(10−3 × 10−9)) ≈ 159 kHz.

  2. Find the wavelength of a 100 MHz radio wave and of a 2.45 GHz microwave. easy

    λ = c/f: 3.0 m and 12.2 cm.

  3. Find the photon energy (in eV) of green light, λ = 500 nm, and an X-ray, λ = 0.1 nm. medium

    E = hc/λ: green ≈ 4.0 × 10−19 J = 2.5 eV. X-ray ≈ 12,400 eV (12.4 keV), enough to ionise.

  4. An EM wave has B = 10−8 T. Find E. easy

    E = cB = 3 × 108 × 10−8 = 3 V/m.

  5. Find the proton Larmor frequency in a 1.5 T and a 3 T scanner. easy

    f = 42.58 × B: 63.9 MHz and 127.7 MHz (radio waves).

  6. A gradient coil makes the field 1.500 T at the head and 1.501 T 10 cm lower. What is the difference in Larmor frequency? medium

    Δf = 42.58 MHz/T × 0.001 T = 42.6 kHz. The scanner uses this difference to tell the two places apart.

  7. Which of α, β, γ would be bent by a magnetic field, and which is most dangerous inside the body? easy

    α and β are charged, so they bend (opposite ways); γ does not. α is most dangerous inside the body because it ionises very strongly over a short distance.
